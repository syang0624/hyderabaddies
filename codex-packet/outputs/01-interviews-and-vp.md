## VP conversation: 10–15 minutes including interpretation

For a ten-minute slot, use minutes0–7 below, spend minutes7–9 on one scale/ownership question, then close at minute9–10 with “Who could show us an example tomorrow?” The future-direction and extended close blocks are optional only if he offers more time.

One interviewer leads; the other takes notes and watches the clock. Ask one short question, allow interpretation and the entire answer, then follow the answer. Do not race through this list. Five substantive questions may be the maximum with interpretation. Permission to record should be requested plainly; don't assume the prior glasses capture covers this interview.

**0:00–1:00 — permission and frame.** “Thank you for making time after your journey. We can keep this to ten minutes. We're exploring how work could change, and haven't committed to a solution. Is taking notes [or recording] okay?”

**1:00–4:00 — open discovery.** “What recurring problem in the teams you oversee most recently needed your intervention?” Follow with “Could you walk us through that instance?” If too broad: “What was the last decision or handoff that took longer than you wanted?” Avoid beginning with your Salesforce theory.

**4:00–7:00 — consequence and current solution.** “What happened because of that delay or difficulty?” Then “How do you handle it today, and what is still unresolved?” Ask for an actual consequence: missed opportunity, manager hours, rework, employee/customer experience. Accept that an existing process works.

**7:00–10:00 — scale and ownership.** Choose what fits the story: “Roughly how often does this happen?” “Which teams experience it?” “Who would have the actual time or cost figures?” If they offer a number: “Is that for this team or the whole organization, and over what period?” Do not demand precision the VP cannot know.

**10:00–12:00 — future direction.** “If AI became much more capable over the next three years, what responsibility would you want it to take on—and what would you insist people retain?” This invites ambition after grounding it in actual work. If the answer is abstract: “Where would you first try that?”

**12:00–15:00 — testability and close.** “What result would justify a small pilot, and who should we learn from next?” If appropriate: “Could that person show us one recent example tomorrow?” Summarize their actual priority in one sentence and invite correction. Optional only if time: “Shion described [one neutral workflow]. Is that representative of a bigger priority, or relatively minor?”

If the story points elsewhere, follow it. The aim is not endorsement of candidate packets, employee clones, or the team's favorite demo.

## Exact wording to quantify time and cost

Start without adopting the interviewer's earlier premise: “You mentioned sharing was difficult. Thinking of the last batch, what work did you personally do after Claude produced the draft?” Then: “Roughly how many minutes did that take, including checking and corrections?” “How many packets were in that batch?” “How often do you do a batch like that?” “Was this typical, or unusually difficult?”

For expense: “When you say expensive, do you mean software spend, people's time, or the consequence of something being delayed?” If software: “Is that an additional cost caused by this workflow, or a system you already need anyway?” Do not count the whole Salesforce bill as savings when Salesforce remains.

Calculation once actual inputs exist: monthly effort = batches per month × active minutes per batch ÷ 60. If measuring per packet, use packets × minutes per packet instead; do not multiply batch and packet counts twice. Net hours freed = current effort − new preparation/review/correction effort − amortized setup/maintenance. Waiting time is a separate metric, not automatically paid labor saved. Dollar conversion needs an explicitly sourced employer cost per hour and remains an estimate; hours freed are not automatically cash savings.
