## Evidence boundary and revision lineage

This is an independent worker-downside stress test of **R01.1**, read in `round-01.md`; it is not a continuation of rounds 02 or 03. It uses the frozen opportunity, judges and interview-audit reports. No customer discovery, prototype execution, expert approval or learner evaluation happened here. All incidents, characters and UI behavior below are proposed fictional fixtures. “Would” means a requirement to implement and verify, not a feature inspected in this round.

R01.1 made C a private rehearsal with learner-controlled sharing. R04.1 challenges whether that promise survives employer funding. The revised concept is **a learner-owned practice room sponsored first by a university careers or learning program**, with no individual employer telemetry or assessment endpoint. This buyer is a hypothesis, not a discovered customer. Learners can practice through text as well as the visual office, replay freely, and ask what the scenario leaves out. Expert authors approve specific examples and permitted audiences; their personality is not copied.

The fictional lenses use public professional relevance only: Workflow & Scale ([Indeed leadership announcement](https://www.indeed.com/news/releases/indeed-appoints-jim-giles-as-chief-technology-officer?co=US)); Worker Value & Business ([Glassdoor mission](https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/ceo-robert-hohman-talks-about-the-glassdoor-mission/)); Real-time Systems ([contextual game demo repository](https://github.com/GoogleCloudDevRel/next25-retro-tech-revolution)); Enterprise AI Reliability ([enterprise agents event](https://academy.openai.com/public/events/workspace-agents-guidance-for-chatgpt-enterprise-admins-zs4dny9yey)). ## Final spoken script — six minutes

**DRAFTED FOR CARL/STEVEN. FICTIONAL SIMULATION. All demonstrated behavior remains proposed until implemented and inspected.**

<!-- FINAL_START -->
**0:00–0:10 — Title; a fictional intern receives an incident.**

“Work Rehearsal proposes a place to try professional decisions before they carry professional stakes. Practice, question the explanation, and decide what you want to learn next.”

**0:10–0:50 — Problem; learner perspective.**

“Imagine your first engineering internship. Errors are rising. A colleague asks you to restart the service. You have logs, a queue and incomplete context. You know the vocabulary, but not what deserves attention.

“A role description cannot answer every question you have not yet discovered. A mentor can help, but their time is limited. Our hypothesis is that one small rehearsal can make a later conversation more useful. It should help you understand the work, including whether you want to do it.”

**0:50–1:20 — Inspiration; evidence boundary.**

“Our interviews found real recruiting handoffs and product debates. They also revealed effective existing workarounds. Neither interview established demand for a simulated workplace.

“This is a new direction to test: an expert could approve one teaching situation, including its limitations, and let learners experience the decision. We would use approved examples, not observe employees in the background or claim to recreate their minds.”

**1:20–3:00 — Solution; show concept storyboard unless a build is verified.**

“The proposed prototype contains one fictional office and one incident. You inspect the queue, ask a colleague for context and choose an action. The software changes the incident according to visible rules. The model cannot decide afterward that every choice was correct.

“Restart while work is pending, and this fictional system produces a specified consequence. Ask, ‘What did I miss?’ The guide would show the relevant clue and an approved explanation. You can challenge that explanation. If another action is reasonable, the feedback should acknowledge it rather than reward obedience to one preferred phrase.

“Now a judge changes a supported condition: the queue is empty. We repeat the same action. Its consequence and explanation should change consistently. Ask about an unsupported production system and the guide should state its limits.

“The office is optional. A text view would expose the same evidence and commands. We would offer private retries without a timer or employability score. The point is to make a decision understandable, not measure how confidently someone performs in a game.”

**3:00–3:55 — Impact and buyer.**

“Our intended beneficiary is a student who cannot easily access a mentor. We have not demonstrated that benefit. We would compare the rehearsal with the same source material in a document, then ask learners to handle a different case. Appropriate uncertainty and requests for help count as useful behavior.

“We would also check whether the experience creates misleading beliefs about the role. A realistic animation does not make a simplified scenario representative.

“Our first buyer hypothesis is a university careers or learning program sponsoring free access. We would count expert authoring and review time before claiming efficiency. We would not sell individual attempt histories to employers. Whether this model has a sustainable budget remains a question.”

**3:55–4:30 — Differentiation.**

“Forage already offers work simulations, and Delphi makes expertise conversational. A virtual office alone is not our distinction. We propose connecting an actual learner action to an inspectable consequence, then letting the learner question grounded feedback. If a simple branching exercise teaches equally well, we should choose the simpler tool.”

**4:30–5:30 — Technical design and agency.**

“The design separates a versioned scenario package, a deterministic state engine, approved explanations and a dialogue layer. Clear commands change state. Ambiguous commands ask for clarification. Slow or unavailable generation leaves the evidence usable and shows the failure honestly.

“Consent also changes architecture. The learner would see what data leaves the device before starting. Practice would not create an employer dashboard. An expert would approve the material, audience and attribution, and could withdraw future access. We would not promise to erase copies already exported.

“We would test changed conditions, alternative valid actions, unsupported questions and unequal interface friction. These are requirements, not completed evaluations.”

**5:30–6:00 — Roadmap; show three gates.**

“First secure one willing expert and a useful teaching case. Then demonstrate the changing incident and its grounded explanation. Finally test learning, accessibility and buyer demand. If the only willing buyer wants covert screening, we stop that route. The ambition is more access to practice that people control.”

### Final Q&A — six minutes, FICTIONAL SIMULATION

**0:00–0:15 — Worker Value & Business:** “You say consent, but a student seeking work will agree to almost anything. What exactly can they refuse?”

**0:15–1:00 — Presenter:** “They should be able to decline without losing access to recruiting or careers support. Our proposed pilot is a learning activity with an equivalent document available, no individual participation feed to employers, and no requirement to share a trace. Before testing, we would explain model processing, retention and who sees what in ordinary language. That does not solve power imbalance by itself. We need private learner feedback on the invitation, not just a checked consent box. If the sponsoring program ties it to selection, the proposed use has changed and we would decline that pilot.”

**1:00–1:15 — Enterprise AI Reliability:** “What proves that your feedback is not simply an expert's bias, automated and amplified?”

**1:15–2:00 — Presenter:** “Nothing in the current packet proves that. We would distinguish observable incident facts from an author's preferences and disclose the authoring scope. For a chosen action, feedback should explain the tradeoff rather than infer confidence, culture fit or personality. We would ask another qualified reviewer to find reasonable alternative paths and test whether equivalent actions receive equivalent feedback across phrasing. That is a scenario-quality check, not proof of general fairness. If expert reviewers disagree, the disagreement belongs in the teaching material. We should not hide it behind an authoritative answer.”

**2:00–2:15 — Real-time Systems:** “A text option can become a worse product. How do you know the game is not excluding the very learners you want to help?”

**2:15–3:00 — Presenter:** “We would build both views on the same state and action schema, with identical evidence and consequences. The text view must not lose clues embedded only in animation or color. No timing or navigation dexterity would enter the feedback. Then we need people to try the actual interface, including keyboard access and assistive technology, before saying it is accessible. Language variation also needs review; translation alone does not establish equivalence. If the office consumes effort without improving understanding, cut it. A small visual world is a way to orient the learner, not a requirement to prove competence.”

**3:00–3:15 — Workflow & Scale:** “No recruiter data, no ranking, no completion certificate. What does a sponsor get that warrants paying?”

**3:15–4:00 — Presenter:** “A hypothesis: a reusable, reviewed teaching activity that makes mentor sessions more useful. We would ask the careers or learning team what activity it replaces, how much staff time that costs, and what educational evidence would justify a purchase. We have no price or committed budget. A separate, voluntary study could evaluate learning without creating an employer-facing profile, but its design still needs agreement. We should not smuggle analytics back in as the business model. If the sponsor's only valuable output is candidate filtering, our revised concept does not satisfy that buyer.”

**4:00–4:15 — Worker Value & Business:** “You also want an expert to give away judgment. Why would that help the worker rather than replace them?”

**4:15–5:00 — Presenter:** “It might not. We would ask the expert why they want to teach this episode and what use they authorize. The scope is one authored situation, not continuous capture or a claim to everything they know. Attribution and compensation would need an explicit agreement; we have none today. Withdrawal should stop future distribution, with honest limits on exported copies. We must not sell the learner a person who never consented to representation. The value to the expert could be fewer repeated explanations or better mentor discussions, but that remains a hypothesis to test with total authoring effort included.”

**5:00–5:15 — Enterprise AI Reliability:** “An employer screenshots the output and uses it to reject someone. Do your safeguards actually prevent misuse?”

**5:15–6:00 — Presenter:** “They reduce what we produce and expose; they cannot prevent every screenshot or external decision. That is why the output should avoid standardized performance badges, scores and verified attempt histories. It should teach the scenario's reasoning and help learners form their own questions. We should explicitly state that it has not been validated for selection. If a pilot starts collecting screenshots for hiring, we can stop providing that pilot, but cannot undo every copied artifact. The boundary is a real limitation, not a claim that a disclaimer makes the risk disappear.”

## Critique-driven revision and reversal conditions

**Lineage: R01.1 → R04.0 worker-downside challenge → R04.1 learner-owned rehearsal.** This is a script/concept revision, not a code or product change. R01.1's “learners choose what to share” was insufficient under unequal bargaining power. R04.1 avoids recruiter accounts, verified completion and standardized performance exports; it starts with a careers/learning sponsor, keeps a complete text interaction, and distinguishes learning feedback from assessment. The expert author receives scope, attribution and withdrawal terms as requirements, not assumed consent.

Reverse to A if observation identifies recurring material post-Claude checking or permission work, the recipient boundary can be respected, and an actual fictional-data improvement can be shown. Reverse to B if a buyer has repeated unresolved choices that genuinely wait on usable alternatives, and current tools do not solve the delay. Reject C if learners feel pressured to participate, reasonable alternatives receive dogmatic feedback, the game creates avoidable access barriers, learning fails to improve over source material, experts decline the use of their knowledge, or authoring costs overwhelm value. If only hiring-screening buyers will pay, do not quietly relabel practice as a validated test: revisit the concept or choose another direction.

**Round decision: conditional C, with narrower business claims and a real dissent for A. The worker's ability to refuse, question and practice privately is part of the product mechanism, not an ornamental promise.**
