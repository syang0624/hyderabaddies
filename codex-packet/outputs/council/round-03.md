## Final presentation — 6 minutes

**DRAFTED FOR CARL/STEVEN. 650 spoken words, with approximately one minute reserved for deliberate viewing and transition pauses.** This is a conditional concept rehearsal. Replace future tense only for specific behaviors subsequently implemented and inspected.

**0:00–0:45 — Carl, problem.**
“Before a candidate meets an engineer, the engineer needs useful context. That context can be scattered across records and recordings, and the recipient may not have access to the original system. A recruiter we interviewed described exporting Salesforce information to Excel and using corporate Claude to prepare profiles. That matters because she has already solved part of the problem. We should begin where her solution ends, rather than pitch AI summarization as a discovery.”

**0:45–1:15 — Steven, observed versus inferred.**
“We know this workflow exists. We do not yet know the preparation time, error rate, frequency of difficult cases, or willingness to pay for an improvement. Handoff is our hypothesis: a short, sourced briefing that respects its recipient and stays reviewable when evidence changes. We would investigate it by watching one recent handoff, using a redacted reconstruction, and asking what still requires judgment or checking.”

**1:15–2:40 — Carl, demonstration narrative; show the proposed storyboard, then allow viewing pauses.**
“Imagine three fictional source records. One says a student is available on Tuesday; a later note suggests that availability changed. An ordinary polished summary can conceal that disagreement. Our prototype would keep the underlying claims separate, identify their sources, and ask for review rather than silently reconcile them.

“Now we select the intended mentor. The draft would contain approved experience and conversation context. It would omit fields the mentor is not authorized to receive. We would apply that rule before the model sees the drafting context, so the boundary does not depend solely on asking a model to be careful.

“Finally, you would change one permitted fact. We want to show the affected sentence, its replacement, and the evidence behind that replacement. The useful artifact is a draft ready for a human to inspect in an existing handoff. Export-based input and fictional records would be labeled. There would be no claim of a production Salesforce integration.”

**2:40–3:30 — Steven, benefit and business.**
“The buyer hypothesis is recruiting operations. The benefit hypothesis is less hands-on assembly and review, with fewer unsupported or inappropriately shared claims. Our comparison must include the entire job: gathering, prompting, checking, correcting, and preparing the final handoff. We would also count onboarding and policy-maintenance effort. We have no savings figure today.

“For candidates, the intended benefit is accurate representation and fewer unnecessary disclosures. We would not generate a suitability score or claim that a better packet improves hiring outcomes without evidence. If the organization cannot authorize the underlying information for the recipient, this system cannot invent that permission.”

**3:30–4:15 — Carl, alternatives.**
“We compared two more ambitious directions. Live Experiment would turn a conversation into working alternatives, but meeting context already reaches coding tools, and our interviewee already prototypes rapidly. Work Rehearsal would let learners act inside an expert-approved scenario. Existing expertise chat and employer simulations make that a demanding comparison too. A lively world would need to teach something a static case cannot. We do not earn differentiation merely by combining familiar interfaces.”

**4:15–5:20 — Steven, architecture and reliability.**
“The proposed architecture has four stages: approved input, structured claims with source locations, a deterministic recipient filter, and constrained drafting with visible review. We would check missing sources, contradictory dates, removed permissions, and edits that affect one claim but not others. Those checks are proposed, not passed tests.

“A model may propose wording. It cannot create an authorization rule or resolve a factual conflict just because one answer sounds plausible. If extraction fails, the interface should say which source could not be processed. If drafting fails, the permitted evidence should remain visible. A source link helps a reviewer inspect a claim; it does not guarantee that the model interpreted the source correctly.”

**5:20–6:00 — Carl, decision and ambition.**
“Our immediate decision is deliberately small: observe the remaining work, build the narrow behavior, and compare it fairly. If Claude plus a better template wins, we stop the product and keep the useful improvement. If recipient-safe checking is a recurring burden that current tools leave unresolved, we have a concrete entry point. The future of work should include tools that remove a verified burden. Novelty is valuable when it changes the outcome.”

**0:00–1:00 — Workflow & Scale:** “Your incumbent is already installed and approved. Aren't you overlooking the cost of becoming another vendor?”

**Presenter:** “That cost could defeat A even if our draft is slightly better. We would first investigate a reusable configuration inside an existing approved workflow. **1:00–2:00 — Worker Value & Business:** “Why not choose Work Rehearsal? It gives students something they cannot get from a candidate summary.”

**Presenter:** “It may offer more direct student value. But Forage already offers employer-designed simulations, and Delphi already offers interactive expertise. We would compare a rehearsal against the same case as a document with feedback, using a new scenario afterward. We would measure role understanding or demonstrated reasoning, not enjoyment alone. **2:00–3:00 — Real-time Systems:** “Could B be the best hackathon demo even if A has better evidence?”

**Presenter:** “Yes. B has a visually strong interaction: change a constraint and watch functioning alternatives respond. But Granola can already share meeting context with coding tools. We would need to demonstrate more than that chain. The proposed test is whether participants can resolve a particular disputed assumption earlier by trying controlled alternatives. We would compare against a competent operator using existing tools, with similar starting components and the same constraints. If the bottleneck is a decision-maker or getting users into the room, faster rendering does not solve it. A spectacular run would demonstrate capability, not demand.”

**3:00–4:00 — Enterprise AI Reliability:** “Your filter seems easy. What happens when a permitted free-text field contains a prohibited fact?”

**Presenter:** “A field allowlist alone would not be enough for arbitrary free text. For the narrow prototype, we would use reviewed structured records and explicitly state that limitation. We would not claim production protection for unreviewed notes. A wider design needs approved extraction and classification, review of uncertain cases, and tests containing sensitive facts in unexpected locations. Even then, a human data owner must define what a mentor can receive. If we cannot demonstrate that boundary reliably on the allowed inputs, we should reduce the scope or withhold the generated draft.”

**4:00–5:00 — Workflow & Scale:** “How will you avoid designing a benchmark where your product inevitably wins?”

**Presenter:** “Use the observed current process as the starting point and let the operator improve it. Give both conditions the same records, permissions, and quality requirements. Record setup separately from repeat usage. Include review and correction rather than timing only generation. Have the recipient judge usefulness without being told which tool produced it where practical. Report individual cases and failure modes before claiming general savings. With a small pilot we can learn whether a larger evaluation is warranted; we cannot claim universal superiority. We should specify what would count as a loss before seeing the results.”

**5:00–6:00 — Worker Value & Business:** “What is your actual recommendation tonight, and what tomorrow would change it?”

**Presenter:** “Tonight, do not confuse this pitch with permission to spend the whole weekend on A. Prepare one minimal comparison and investigate the remaining task. A meaningful recurring correction problem would justify proceeding. If Shion says her process is quick and trustworthy, stop A. If an expert can approve one useful practice case and a buyer describes a repeated training gap, C deserves the creative prototype slot. If a team shows an actual stalled decision that existing rapid prototypes failed to resolve, B deserves a test. The outcome can also be that none has enough evidence yet. We want the next fact to change our decision.”

## Critique-driven revision and reversal conditions

**Revision 03.0 → 03.1:** Replace “automated candidate handoff” with “reviewable recipient-specific draft”; explicitly permit a template to win. Remove implied completeness of free-text privacy filtering. Separate rubric concept score from evidence-led investigation order. Retain C's genuine creative lead while refusing to transfer Shion's interview validation to C.

Three facts would change the decision:

1. **A loses immediately** if observation shows little residual work, authorization blocks the necessary information, or an improved Claude/template workflow matches the full task. Do not argue the interviewee into pain.
2. **B rises** if a team shows a recurring disputed decision whose bottleneck is trying alternatives during the conversation, and a fair baseline cannot produce the same usable contrast in the required setting. A fast generated screen alone is insufficient.
3. **C rises** if an expert approves a specific practice case, a buyer names a recurring learner problem, and a modest comparison supports action-dependent learning over the same content in a simpler format. If only the world is attractive, use the simpler format.
