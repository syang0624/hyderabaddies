## Final script — 6 minutes; approximately 715 spoken words

[0:00–0:30 — Title and persistent fictional-case badge.]

**Presenter:** “Imagine your first difficult moment in a new job. A colleague is impatient, the evidence is incomplete, and a decision has consequences. Now imagine getting to try that moment before it happens for real. We propose Work Rehearsal: a small simulated workplace where you can ask, inspect, act and see what follows.”

[0:30–1:15 — Show evidence boundary and source card.]

“We began with the ambition of making experienced workers' judgment available to newcomers. But ambition does not give us company data or an expert's time. Under our current constraints, neither is available for this prototype. So the case would be openly fictional, with rules authored for the demonstration. We would not claim it represents a company's procedure or a real mentor's advice.

“Our interviews also showed that people already have useful AI workarounds. They did not establish demand for this training product. That leaves two separate questions: can we build a compelling rehearsal, and will anyone benefit enough to adopt it?”

[1:15–2:40 — Proposed 85-second live sequence; pause for participant action.]

“Here is the single case we propose to build. You enter a tiny office during a fictional service incident. A coworker asks you to restart the service immediately. You can inspect a status card, ask a question, restart or wait.

“The scenario defines one decisive fact: is a batch still active? Under this invented rule, restarting during the batch interrupts it. Once the batch completes, restarting restores the fictional service. These are rules of this exercise, not universal engineering advice.

“Your choice would create an event in the state log. The room would update from that event. Ask why, and the explanation would identify your action, the observed state and the rule that produced the outcome.

“Then a judge would change the batch status and rerun the same action. A different consequence should follow. That is the technical claim we want to demonstrate. If the judge asks about a policy outside the case, the system should say the scenario does not answer it. A fluent invented answer would be a failure.”

[2:40–3:30 — Baselines and buyer hypothesis.]

“Our first buyer hypothesis is a support onboarding manager whose experienced staff repeatedly coach the same decisions. We have not verified that cost or purchasing authority. The proposed value is practice between human conversations, with the expert retaining control of any professional material.

“A short document, branching form or generic chatbot may already be sufficient. We would compare equivalent material and practice time, then use a changed case to test whether learners notice the important evidence. We would count content creation, expert review and maintenance. A prettier scene alone does not justify a training budget.”

[3:30–4:15 — Architecture; no hidden integration logos.]

“The one-day architecture would be small. A versioned scenario file contains the allowed states, actions and explanation rules. Deterministic code computes consequences. The language model answers only from the current state and that rule set; it cannot alter state by narration.

“Buttons would perform the consequential actions. Natural-language action parsing, company connectors, voice cloning and multiple departments are outside this build. If model access fails, the learner can still inspect the rule and finish the case. That fallback would demonstrate resilience, not successful AI inference.”

[4:15–5:05 — Social value and limits.]

“The broader hope is that more people can experience unfamiliar work without needing a personal introduction to someone already inside it. Existing employer simulations and interactive expertise products show that this is an established area, so we cannot call the category new. Our proposed distinction is the visible connection between a person's decision, a changed environment and a grounded explanation.

“Learners would be able to retry. Their mistakes would remain practice artifacts, not hidden hiring scores. We would not infer personality, cultural fit or employability from a synthetic coworker's reaction. Whether the playful interface helps people learn remains an empirical question.”

[5:05–6:00 — Build gate and professional-content gate.]

“First, prove one transition and its changed-condition rerun. Then prove the explanation stays inside its source. Only then add the room's visual polish. If those behaviors do not run, we should present a concept honestly.

“After the event, the professional pilot requires a manager with a recurring problem and an expert willing to review a case. We can draft material to reduce their effort, but their silence cannot count as approval. If qualified review is unavailable, the product remains fictional practice. If a plain chatbot teaches equally well, or review cost overwhelms reuse, we should change direction. The ambition is broad access to experience. The first proof is deliberately small.”

## Critique-driven revision and reversal conditions

Version 2.0 pitched one expert-approved onboarding scenario. Version 5.0 removes that approval claim, separates a fictional mechanism proof from a professional pilot, makes deterministic buttons the action surface, caps the room to one coworker, adds a fixed authoritative result card and puts content review back into the commercial risk. It preserves the changed-clue moment and learner control. It lowers C's impact score rather than treating missing enterprise access as an advantage.

Choose A if a permitted observation establishes meaningful recurring work after Claude and a recipient/buyer wants a bounded test, or if its actual mechanism is much more ready at the build gate. Choose B if an existing functioning kit supports honest judge-driven variation and a real recurring decision bottleneck emerges. Neither alternative requires enterprise data merely to demonstrate software, but each needs real workflow evidence to justify adoption.

Stop or reframe C if the core transition is not running by the early build gate; if the explanation cannot remain faithful, show direct rules and narrow the technical claim. Stop the professional-training direction if no qualified reviewer will participate, review/maintenance outweighs reuse, or equivalent baseline content performs as well on a fresh case. Continue beyond the hackathon only when an actual problem owner, willing reviewer and pre-agreed evaluation exist. No mentor response means no approval, however compelling the demo becomes.
