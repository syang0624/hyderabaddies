02 Problem

At Recruit Holdings, AI is blurring every role, yet task forces are still picked from memory.

Who

Anyone staffing a task: team leads, HR, and every colleague who needs to loop someone in.

What

Finding who has done this and who wants to, for every new task. Will has no metric.

Why now

With AI, HR can ship code and engineers can run HR. Fixed teams give way to one task force per task.

80.9% of employees in Japan already use AI at work

Why current options fall short

Directories and skill tags end in intuition, so the same few people get every task.

one task force, per task

Recruit Management Solutions, Jan 2026, n=1,429 · interviews at Recruit, roles only

Read the title as one breath. Five roles sit on the right; on the third keypress they merge into one task force.

Keypress 1, Who: the end users are everyone who staffs a task: the team lead forming it, HR placing people, and any colleague who wants to loop in the right person. Not only HR: anyone who asks “who has done this before?”.

Keypress 2, What: internal mobility, team formation and career growth come down to one question per task: who has actually done this, and who wants to? At Recruit, will is asked in the Will Can Must 1:1 twice a year and never measured; an HR leader told us there is no metric for it and matching runs on intuition.

Keypress 3, Why now: the five roles merge into one task force. With AI, an HR person can build software and an engineer can do HR work; Recruit already works this way (“CEO-ship”, job boundaries are blurry, the head of HR told us). So staffing becomes per task, and the question needs an answer inside the tools already in use: 80.9% of employees in Japan already use AI at work (Recruit Management Solutions, Jan 2026, n=1,429). A new tool forced on everyone at once fails.

Keypress 4, Why current options fall short: company directories and skill tags still end in intuition, “in the order they come to mind”, so the best-known person gets every task and burns out while others are never asked. Reading work data one-sidedly is not the answer either: the head of HR called that “really creepy”.

To Jim: one task force per task is skills-first staffing, a workflow he has run at scale. To Robert: the same few people get every task because nobody can see what the others have done; that asymmetry is the problem. To Damien: 80.9% AI use at work is why this must run inside the tools people already use, on the customer’s cloud. To Ho Joon: the failure mode is a wrong AI output (a drifting skill tag) driving a people decision.

A2 Is that a score?

No. It is a confidence for one task, from a person’s own evidence.

The engine computes

coverage = words of the ask with evidence from this person ÷ words in the ask

Never

Sorted into a rank, or shown to the person as a number. Tiles are listed A to Z.

prototype/engine.py, coverage(): “2 of 7 words in the ask have evidence from this person”, per ask only

Is that a score? No. “2 of 7 words have evidence” is coverage of the ask by that person’s own evidence, never sorted by, never shown to the person as a rank. It changes with the ask, not with the person.

To Jim: coverage is per ask, so there is no standing profile to game. To Robert: the person is never shown a number about themselves. To Damien: the computation is deterministic code over the model’s cited claims. To Ho Joon: this is the design decision that removes the ranking incentive from the model.

A11 The state model

A retrieval model over evidence, never a prediction about a person.

What it learns

What each person has done, wants and can take on, as a time-aware vector, from allowed evidence only.

How it is trained

Contrastive retrieval: asks paired with the evidence filed decisions used; contests and dropped claims as corrections.

Evaluated by recall of decision-used evidence on held-out cycles, contest rate, dropped-claim share.

Retrieval objective, not a prediction about a person; no protected attributes; the sourced claim is the unit; a human decides.

The state model in detail: a time-aware vector per person, learned only from allowed evidence and opted-in outcomes. Trained by contrastive retrieval (an ask paired with the evidence a filed decision used); contests and dropped claims are corrections; no protected attributes; drift is audited. Evaluated on held-out past cycles: recall of the evidence decisions used, contest rate, dropped-claim share.

To Ho Joon: the model retrieves evidence and is trained on the organisation’s own decisions; the output is a set of quotes, so a wrong model shows a wrong source line, which the person can contest, never a wrong score.
